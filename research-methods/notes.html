Principles of Scientific Research

Scientific Thinking · Empirical Cycle · Validity · GenAI in Research

Scientific Thinking & Cognitive Biases

Scientists must actively resist cognitive shortcuts that distort reasoning. The first principle: "You must not fool yourself – and you are the easiest person to fool."

Confirmation Bias

Tendency to look for evidence that confirms your belief and ignore disconfirming evidence. (Card-flipping problem — people flip the vowel and the even number, but should flip the vowel AND the odd number.)

Availability Heuristic

Judge frequency by how easily examples come to mind. Words starting with "r" seem more common than words with "r" in the 3rd position — but there are actually more of the latter.

Representativeness Heuristic

Judge probability by how much something resembles a prototype. Arthur sounds like a librarian, but there are far more cashiers than librarians — base rate neglect.

Hindsight Bias

"I knew it all along." After learning an outcome, we believe we predicted it. Distorts evaluation of research.

Exam Tip
Be able to match each bias to its example from the slides (card/vowel = confirmation, r-words = availability, Arthur = representativeness).

Phase 3 (Deduction) = making a testable hypothesis from a theory. If the question says "if the hypothesis is true, then…" — that is Deduction, not Observation.

Validity — Are Our Claims Correct?

External Validity

Do findings generalise beyond this sample/setting/time? Threatened by biased sampling (e.g., convenience sampling, non-response bias).

Internal Validity

Is the causal story correct? Are there confounds or alternative explanations? Threatened by extraneous variables, poor design.

Confounding Variables

A confound (third variable) causes a spurious association between two variables. Classic example:

Ice cream sales ↑ → Shark attacks ↑ — but Temperature drives both. This pattern is called common response. Correlation ≠ causation.

Study Types

Correlational Study

  • Measure variables, see how they relate
  • Cannot establish causation
  • Alternative explanations remain

Experimental Study

  • Manipulate variables (IV)
  • Random assignment to conditions
  • Rules out most alternatives
  • Stronger internal validity

Within vs Between Subjects

DesignWhat it meansProsConsSolution
Within subjects (repeated measures)Each participant does ALL conditionsFewer participants needed; controls individual differencesOrder effects (training, fatigue)Counterbalancing
Between subjectsEach participant does ONE conditionNo contamination from other conditionsPre-existing group differencesRandom assignment

Sampling Methods

Simple Random Sampling

Every member of population has equal chance of selection. More likely to generalise. Not always feasible.

Convenience Sampling

Use whoever is easiest to recruit (e.g., students). Quicker/cheaper, but less generalisable; risk of bias.

GenAI in Research

  • GenAI is not an author — it cannot take responsibility.
  • Policies vary across journals; many require a statement on genAI use.
  • In this course: only allowed for spelling/grammar/style. Must disclose prompts and reflect.

Data Collection

Variables & Metrics · Collection Methods · Annotation · Research Proposal

Variables & Measurement

In research we measure variables. In supervised ML these split into:

Features (X)

Predictor / input variables used to make predictions.

Target / Label (Y)

What we want to predict or are primarily interested in.

Quantitative / Numerical

Numerical values (continuous or discrete) allowing arithmetic operations.

Qualitative / Categorical

Labels and ranks. Note: qualitative ≠ always categorical (e.g., interview data).

Requirements for Variables

Exhaustive

Fully covers what is being measured — every possible answer has a category.

Mutually Exclusive

No overlap between categories — each observation belongs to exactly one category.

Exam Tip
A bad set of categories (easy, fast, popular) fails both — they overlap and leave out many options. A good set adds an "Other: ___" option and assigns languages to exactly one category.

Structured vs Unstructured Data

Structured (Tabular)

Clear relationships between variables. Think spreadsheet: each row = unit of analysis, each column = variable. height(Leia) = 150.

Unstructured

No clear schema — images, free text, audio. ~80–90% of corporate data growth. Requires annotation or feature extraction.

Self-Report Sub-Types

Questionnaire

A tool (not a method). Structured set of questions; written or digital; can be self-administered or researcher-administered; yields quantitative data but can include open-ended items.

Survey

A method / design strategy. Broader than questionnaire. Used to collect data from a sample and generalise to a population. Can include both questionnaires and interviews.

Interview

Direct interaction. Ranges from structured (fixed questions, like a questionnaire) → semi-structured (guided but flexible) → unstructured (open, conversational). Often qualitative.

Response Formats

Open-ended questions

Not leading; rich info; but harder to code and analyse.

Rating scales (Likert)

Easy to use/process. Choices: number of points (4, 5, 7…) and labels (yes/no vs degree wording).

Multiple choice

Depends on response options chosen. Risk: options don't cover all possibilities.

Response Biases

Social Desirability Bias

Participants answer the way they think looks good, not honestly. Solutions: anonymity, indirect questioning, self-administration.

Acquiescence Bias ("Yea-saying")

Tendency to agree regardless of content. Even LLMs show this! Solutions: reverse-scored items, neutral language, control questions.

Behavioural Observation Details

By Setting

  • Naturalistic: real-life context, behaviour as it occurs naturally
  • Contrived: artificial setting (lab or field experiment)

By Concealment

  • Unconcealed: participants know they're observed → reactivity
  • Concealed: unaware/deceived → ethical concerns
  • Solutions: partial concealment, knowledgeable informants, unobtrusive measures

Annotation

AI models often need labelled data. Annotators/coders/raters are humans who provide that context.

Crowdworking Platforms

Amazon Mechanical Turk, Prolific, Appen — collect labels at scale. Useful for large datasets where expert labelling is costly.

Risks of Crowdworking

Low-quality labels (careless responses), unclear instructions → inconsistent annotations, demographic bias in annotator pool.

Solutions

Multiple annotations per item, inter-rater reliability checks, qualification/training, gold standard checks, expert review.

Exam Tip
Interval has NO meaningful zero (0°C is not "no temperature"). Ratio HAS a meaningful zero (0 seconds = no time elapsed). You can say "twice as long" for ratio but not for interval.

Discrete vs Continuous

Discrete

Limited number of possible values (integers). E.g., number of words in a response. R: sample(), rpois().

Continuous

Infinite possible values in a range. E.g., response latency in seconds. R: rnorm(), runif().

Probability Distributions

Probability Mass Function (PMF)

For discrete distributions. Vertical lines at each possible value; height = probability. All heights sum to 1.

Probability Density Function (PDF)

For continuous distributions. Probability of any single exact value = 0. Area under the curve for a range = probability. Total area = 1.

Exam Tip
Know when to use each distribution. Poisson → count data (non-negative integers, no upper bound). Normal → continuous, bell-shaped, real-valued. Uniform → all values equally likely.

Simulating with the Linear Model

y = b₀ + b₁·x₁ + b₂·x₂ + ... + ε
  • b₀ = intercept (baseline value of y when all x = 0)
  • b₁, b₂… = regression coefficients (how much y changes per unit change in x)
  • ε (epsilon) = error / noise — often simulated as rnorm(n, 0, sd). Represents unexplained variation / residuals.
  • Positive coefficient → x increases → y increases. Negative → inverse relationship.

Chatbot example: frustration = −2 + 3·latency + 0.1·great_question − 0.01·response_length + 2·(emojis=="yes") + ε

Recoding & Transformations

  • Clamping/clipping: pmin(x, 10), pmax(x, 0) — enforce upper/lower bounds
  • Rounding: round(x) — convert to integers
  • Power transformation: x^2 creates a right-skewed distribution from a normal one
  • Thresholding: x > 5 converts continuous to binary (TRUE/FALSE)

Logistic Model (for Binary Outcomes)

When the outcome is binary (yes/no, 0/1), model the probability of the outcome instead of the outcome directly.

π = 1 / (1 + e−(b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂))   (Sigmoid function)

Equivalently (the log-odds form, which is linear):

log(π / (1−π)) = b₀ + b₁x₁ + b₂x₂
  • Maps from (−∞, +∞) → (0, 1) — always gives a valid probability
  • Intercept controls the average probability — higher intercept = higher probabilities overall
  • Range of linear predictor controls how extreme probabilities get — larger range = more extreme probabilities
  • To get binary outcome from probabilities: use sample(c("no","yes"), prob=c(1−π, π))
Exam Tip
Logistic model does NOT include noise/ε in the linear predictor (the randomness comes from sampling yes/no with the computed probability).

Why Simulate Data?

Evaluate/compare methods

Test how a statistical method or algorithm performs under known conditions.

Test an analysis plan

Fake pilot data — try out your pipeline before real data is collected.

Education / illustration

Demonstrate concepts cleanly without real-world messiness.

Benchmarks

Evaluate models with commonly used datasets (MNIST, iris) or synthetic data.

Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

Look at patterns without modelling or testing. Essential first step — reveals outliers, errors, deviations from assumptions, inspires hypotheses.

Warning: If you form hypotheses by exploring data, then test those hypotheses on the same data, you inflate Type I error. Spurious patterns look significant (see spurious correlations).

Key Descriptive Statistics

Mean

Average value. Sensitive to outliers.

Median

Value of the middle observation. Robust to outliers.

Variance (s²)

s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n−1). Average squared deviation from mean.

Standard Deviation (s)

s = √s². Same unit as the variable.

Quartiles / IQR

Q1 (25th pct), Q2 (median, 50th pct), Q3 (75th pct). IQR = Q3−Q1. Outliers in boxplot: >1.5×IQR outside box.

Pearson Correlation (r)

r = cov(x,y) / (s_x · s_y). Ranges −1 to +1. Measures linear association.

Exam Tip
Spearman correlation uses ranks instead of actual values → more robust to outliers and non-linear monotone relationships.

Dimension Reduction & Clustering

PCA (linear)

Principal Component Analysis — prcomp() in R. Finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance.

t-SNE / UMAP (non-linear)

Better for visualising cluster structure in high-dimensional data. Non-linear.

Hierarchical Clustering

Agglomerative — hclust(). Builds a dendrogram. Unsupervised.

k-means Clustering

kmeans(). Assigns observations to k clusters by minimising within-cluster variance.

Confirmatory (Inferential) Analysis

We use a sample to make inferences about a population. Because sampling is random, we could be wrong.

Frequentist Hypothesis Testing

Assume H₀ (null hypothesis)
→
Collect data, compute test statistic
→
Calculate p-value
→
If p < α → reject H₀
p-value (definition)

The probability of observing a test statistic this extreme or more extreme, assuming H₀ is true. It is NOT the probability that H₀ is true.

α (significance threshold)

Conventionally α = 0.05. This is the acceptable Type I error rate — probability of falsely rejecting H₀.

Exam Tip — p-value statement
The p-value is a statement about the data (or test statistic), NOT about the null hypothesis or the alternative hypothesis. If H₀ is true, p-values are uniformly distributed (flat) from 0 to 1.

Types of Error

Type I Error (α)

Reject H₀ when H₀ is true. False positive. "Find an effect that isn't there." Probability = α.

Type II Error (β)

Fail to reject H₀ when H₀ is false. False negative. "Miss a real effect." Power = 1 − β.

Confidence Intervals

Rather than just reporting point estimates, report 95% confidence intervals. If 0 is not in the CI for a coefficient, it is statistically significant at α = 0.05.

Bayesian vs Frequentist Probability

Frequentist

  • Truth is fixed; hypotheses have no probabilities
  • Only sample statistics are random variables
  • Uses p-values and confidence intervals
  • Most common in practice

Bayesian

  • Probability = degree of belief
  • Probabilities for everything (models, parameters)
  • Updates prior beliefs with data → posterior
  • Uses Bayes Factors, posterior distributions

Prediction & Classification

Models for inference (like logistic regression) can also be used for prediction. Other common classifiers:

Random Forest

Ensemble of decision trees. Uses majority vote. Less interpretable ("black box"). randomForest().

k-Nearest Neighbours (k-NN)

Classify by majority vote of k nearest training examples. knn3() in caret. k=1 → overfitting!

Neural Net

Layers of connected nodes. nnet(). Flexible but needs scaling of inputs and careful tuning.

Confusion Matrix & Metrics

Actual: Positive (Spam)Actual: Negative (Not spam)
Predicted: Positive True Positive (TP) False Positive (FP) — Type I
Predicted: Negative False Negative (FN) — Type II True Negative (TN)
Accuracy

(TP + TN) / Total. Misleading with class imbalance!

Precision

TP / (TP + FP). Of all predicted positives, how many are real?

Recall (Sensitivity)

TP / (TP + FN). Of all actual positives, how many did we find?

F1 Score

2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall). Harmonic mean; good when classes are imbalanced.

Overfitting

Overfitting: Model fits training data perfectly (e.g., k-NN with k=1 → perfect training accuracy) but generalises poorly to new data. More complex models → better training fit, not necessarily better test performance. Solutions: train/validation/test split, cross-validation.

Open & Reproducible Research

Open Data · Computational Reproducibility · Replication · CS/AI Issues · Publication Process

Open Data

Technology enables easy sharing of data, materials, code, notes. This is valuable for: evaluating scientific contributions, reusability by others.

  • Major journals (Nature, Science) and top AI conferences (NeurIPS, AAAI) require data/code sharing upon publication.
  • Despite policies: ~27–38% of authors don't share data when requested.
  • Reasons for not sharing: data lost/inaccessible, collaborator left, privacy rules, no time, proprietary constraints, copyright issues.
Exam Tip
Know the difference between sharing obligation vs actual compliance rates (quite low in practice).

Computational Reproducibility

Definition

The possibility to obtain the same results and claims using the same data and same analytical procedures.

Even without access to data, reproducibility can be partially assessed:

  • statcheck.io — recalculates p-values from test statistics and degrees of freedom; reported p ≠ recalculated p is a red flag.
  • SPRITE (rsprite) — checks if reported mean and SD of integer data are mathematically possible given the sample size.

Causes of Irreproducibility

  • Copy-pasting errors, manual rounding
  • Changes through review-revision cycles
  • Inadvertent omission of crucial information
  • Questionable research practices (QRPs)
  • Fraud
  • No random seed specified for simulations

Solutions

  • Co-pilot (second person independently checking analysis)
  • Reproducibility check by journals/reviewers
  • Literate programming tools: Jupyter Notebook, Rmarkdown, Quarto — code and text are integrated

Replication

Definition (NSF)

The ability to duplicate the results of a prior study if the same procedures are followed but new data are collected.

Key distinction: Computational reproducibility = same data + same code = same result. Replication = same procedure + new data = same result.

Replication Prevalence (it's low!)

Field% of articles that are replications
Psychology~0.2–1.07%
Economics~0.1%
Ecology & Evolution~0.02%
Software Engineering<0.01%

Why so few replications?

  • Bad reputation: successful = "nothing new"; failed = "personal attack"
  • Costly; not enough computing power/time/money
  • Information not available for replication

Replication Success Rates

  • Psychology (Open Science Collaboration 2015): only ~36–64% of findings replicated
  • Nutritional epidemiology: very low replication success (many reversals)

Explanations for Low Success

  • Fraud, QRPs, different procedures (incomplete reporting), poor computational reproducibility

Reproducibility Issues Specific to CS/AI

In CS/AI, the distinction between computational reproducibility and replication can blur (library updates, hardware differences, "what is new data?").

Data Splitting Issues

Confounds in Splits

"We took the last 10% as test set" — temporal ordering may confound results. Solution: verify split properties are similar; random shuffle before splitting.

Underspecification

Reporting "50/25/25 split" without code → not reproducible. Solution: Share exact code for how splits were created.

Leakage

Test/validation info "leaks" into training (e.g., preprocessing on full data before split, overlapping examples). Solution: Split FIRST, then preprocess. Inspect data carefully.

Metric Issues

Class Imbalance

Accuracy can be misleading when one class dominates. F1, Precision, Recall are more robust. Example: 85% accuracy on spam detection might just be classifying everything as "not spam."

Seed Selection

Non-deterministic processes give different results with different seeds. Solution: seed averaging (run with multiple seeds, average performance).

Exam Tip — Class imbalance worked example
If 140 "not spam" and 30 spam: accuracy = (30+140)/200 = 85%, but F1 = 67%. These tell very different stories about model quality.

Publication Process

1. Identify outlet
Be wary of predatory journals (no quality control, fast, charge fees) and paper mills.
2. Editor assesses fit
Can lead to desk reject (not sent for review), or sent to 1–5 peer reviewers.
3. Peer review
Single-blind (reviewers anonymous), double-blind (both anonymous), or open. Reviewers generally don't rerun analyses. Not paid.
4. Editorial decision
Accept as-is, minor revision, major revision, or reject. Publication bias: positive results more likely accepted → file-drawer effect.
5. Revision & response
Authors revise. Response letter: thank editor/reviewers, address each point, repeat until accepted or rejected.

Peer Review Details

  • Single-blind: reviewer anonymous, author identity known to reviewer.
  • Double-blind: both parties anonymous.
  • Reviews can be published openly.
  • Reviewers generally do not assess reproducibility (don't rerun analyses).
  • Potential for scooping and other fraudulent practices (e.g., plagiarism by reviewer).
Publication bias + File-drawer effect: Statistically significant / positive results are more publishable. Studies with null results languish in file drawers, skewing the literature.

Ethical failures: hurt participants, create shoddy science, damage public trust in science. Historical example: Tuskegee Syphilis Experiment (1932–1972) — patients not informed of diagnosis, treatment withheld for 40 years → directly led to the Belmont Report and Institutional Review Boards (IRBs).

The Belmont Framework

Three core ethical principles (also exists: Helsinki Declaration, APA ethics, Hippocratic Oath):

1. Respect for Persons

Treat people as autonomous agents. Provide more protection to those at risk (children, vulnerable groups). Application: informed consent.

2. Beneficence

Beyond "do no harm" — actively consider risks to participants, both immediate and long-term. Application: risk-benefit assessment.

3. Justice

Equitable and fair distribution of benefits and burdens. Counter manipulation, coercion, and biases. Application: fair subject selection.

Informed Consent — 3 Conditions

1. Information

Participants must know what they are agreeing to.

2. Comprehension

Information must be understood (not just given).

3. Voluntariness

Participation must be free — no coercion. Genuine option to decline.

Risk-Benefit Assessment

  • Evaluate: kinds of risk (physical, psychological, social, legal)
  • Evaluate: severity and likelihood of risks
  • Done before the study — prospective, not retrospective
Belmont Checklist for every study:
1. Was consent valid?   2. Were the risks justified?   3. Was subject selection fair?

Case Study: Facebook Emotional Contagion Experiment (2014)

Facebook randomly assigned users to see more positive or more negative content in their news feed, then analysed their posts for emotional tone (published in PNAS 2014).

Belmont PrincipleWhat happenedWhat should have happened
ConsentNothing disclosed; Terms of Service did not cover research participation; no opportunity to decline.Opt-in recruitment with genuine informed consent.
Risk-BenefitNo systematic risk assessment; effect on depressed users not considered; even small effect sizes at Facebook's scale = huge aggregate impact.Prospective harm assessment; monitoring during study.

Exam Tip
Facebook argued the ToS covered it — but the ToS did NOT include a clause about being used in experiments. The absence of IRB review was a key failing.

Ethics in Practice

1. Collection & Scraping

  • Check robots.txt — specifies what bots may/may not access
  • Respect rate limits — don't overload servers
  • Read the Terms of Service

2. Data Storage & GDPR

In the EU, personal data is protected by law (GDPR):

  • Must have a lawful basis (consent, legitimate interest, or legal obligation)
  • Collect only what is necessary (data minimisation)
  • Set retention periods; control access
Pseudonymisation

Identity replaced (e.g., name → robotkiller123) but a key still exists. Still considered personal data → GDPR applies.

Anonymisation

Identity cannot be recovered (redaction, aggregation). Not personal data → GDPR does not apply.

3. Analysis & Questionable Research Practices (QRPs)

Selective Reporting

Only reporting the significant/positive results from many analyses run on the same data.

p-hacking

Running many tests / tweaking analyses until p < 0.05. Inflates Type I error.

HARKing

Hypothesising After Results are Known. Presenting post-hoc findings as if they were pre-planned hypotheses.

Multiverse analysis (Steegen et al., 2016): Running all reasonable analysis choices and reporting all outcomes, increasing transparency.

AI Ethics

Where Things Go Wrong in AI

  • Data labellers often work under poor conditions for low pay
  • Training data carries the biases of whoever collected it
  • Deployed models are hard to audit or contest
  • Large models have real environmental cost

Beneficence

Harm testing before deployment.

Social scoring, manipulative AI, emotional inference without consent.

Ethical Data Visualisation

"A misleading figure is a misleading claim." Visualisation is part of analysis and must be reproducible and honest.

Common Bad Visualisation Practices

  • Truncated y-axis — exaggerates differences
  • 3D charts — distort proportions via perspective
  • No uncertainty shown — hiding variability
  • Combined plots that mix scales or apples with oranges

Colourblind-Friendly Design

  • Use palettes: viridis, Okabe-Ito, ColorBrewer
  • Test with colorblindcheck R package or colorblindness simulation websites

🎯 Quick-fire Exam Cheat Sheet

Empirical cycle Phase 3 = ?

Deduction — making a testable hypothesis FROM a theory.

Internal vs External validity

Internal = causal story correct. External = generalises to other settings/populations.

Within vs Between subjects

Within = everyone does all conditions (use counterbalancing). Between = different groups per condition (use random assignment).

Questionnaire vs Survey

Questionnaire = a tool. Survey = a method/design strategy.

Type I vs Type II error

I = reject true H₀ (false positive, prob = α). II = keep false H₀ (false negative, prob = β).

p-value is NOT…

Not the probability H₀ is true. It's P(data this extreme | H₀ true).

Reproducibility vs Replication

Reproducibility = same data + same code. Replication = new data + same procedure.

Leakage in ML

Test set info contaminating training. Prevent by: split data FIRST, then preprocess.

Belmont 3 principles

Respect for Persons (→ consent), Beneficence (→ risk-benefit), Justice (→ fair selection).

Pseudonymisation vs Anonymisation

Pseudo = reversible, still personal data (GDPR applies). Anon = irreversible (GDPR doesn't apply).

HARKing

Hypothesising After Results are Known. A QRP.
